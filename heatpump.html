Assumptions & methodology

Every number above is a modelled estimate — here's exactly how it's built, and what to distrust.

Show assumptions — how this is calculated, and what to distrust

This tool runs a transparent, dependency-free hour-by-hour simulation (8,760 hours) of a single typical year, entirely in your browser. It is a screening tool: good for understanding direction and rough magnitude, not a substitute for a heat-loss assessment or a quote. Full derivations, factors and validation live in HeatPump/METHODOLOGY.md in the repository.

Heating load

Each hour's heat demand is Load = UA × max(0, T_balance − T_outdoor). UA (heat-loss coefficient) and the balance-point temperature are derived per city × archetype from NRCan EnerGuide (ERS) audit records — median design heat loss, floor area and pre-retrofit annual heating energy — calibrated so the reconstructed annual load reproduces the audited median within ±10%. Values describe an as-found, un-retrofitted home. Domestic hot water is excluded.

Weather

A CWEC2020 Typical Meteorological Year (8,760 hourly temperatures) for each city's airport station (ECCC engineering climate datasets).

Heat-pump performance

Capacity and COP versus outdoor temperature are digitized from each representative unit's primary public manufacturer datasheet (submittal / product data), at maximum heating output (what a cold home calling for full heat draws), converted to SI. Each tier is the average of two real, publicly-documented models: premium = Mitsubishi MUZ-FH12NAH (H2i) + PUZ-HA36NKA (hyper-heat); mid-market = Carrier Infinity 25HNB9 (2-stage) + Daikin DZ20VC (variable-speed); baseline = Carrier 25HNB5 (single-stage) + Mitsubishi MUZ-HM12NA2. Average installed maps to the baseline curve. Below the coldest published point, capacity is extrapolated and COP floored; by default output is zero below each unit's published minimum operating temperature (compressor lock-out). Carrier's tables are already defrost-integrated; the others get a 7% defrost derate to COP between −7 and +4 °C.

The published minimum (e.g. −25 °C for the premium tier) is a manufacturer's warranty/ratings floor, not a hard physical stop. An Advanced toggle, “continue derated below minimum”, lets the pump keep running colder — capacity extrapolated on the coldest-segment slope, COP held at the floor. This is genuinely manufacturer-unspecified territory (no datasheet publishes it), so it is off by default and clearly labelled where on.
A sizing sweep card re-runs the whole year at 40–160% of the home's design heat loss so you can see how emissions, cost, seasonal COP and backup reliance respond to under/oversizing. It has no short-cycling model, so it understates the penalty of oversizing (short-cycling and worse part-load efficiency) — oversizing looks nearly free on that chart but isn't in a real home.
The tiers differ mainly in cold-weather capability — the premium units hold ~100% of rated capacity down to −15 °C and run to −25 °C, while baseline units shed capacity and lock out around −15 to −19 °C. Peak efficiency (COP) is similar across all modern units; the premium advantage is needing far less backup on the coldest hours. Tier curves are two-model averages, not a specific model you can buy.

How the equipment is chosen — rebuilt 2026

The tiers above are being replaced. The old ones were buckets of a third-party vendor product list, with boundaries of our own invention. The new method separates two jobs that should never have been one: AHRI certification is the sampling frame; manufacturer datasheets are the measurement.

Which models matter comes from Canada's own EnerGuide/ERS audit records — every AHRI certified reference number ever entered by an energy advisor, 439,975 record appearances across 15,148 distinct certified models. So the answer to “why these heat pumps?” is simply: these are the ones Canadians actually bought. Not a vendor list, not a hand-picked shortlist.

How they are bucketed uses two ratings from each unit's public AHRI certificate, on a 3×3 grid:

  • COP at 5 °F — ≤ 1.8 / 1.8–2.0 / > 2.0. Efficiency in deep cold. The bands are deliberately coarse: 1.75 is the certification floor shared by ENERGY STAR, CEE and NEEP, and 55% of records report exactly 1.80, so this number measures compliance as much as performance and should not be read as a fine-grained ranking.
  • Capacity maintenance — capacity at 5 °F ÷ capacity at 47 °F, banded < 0.60 / 0.60–0.80 / ≥ 0.80. How much heat the unit still delivers when you need it most. This is not our metric: ENERGY STAR v6.2, CEE and NRCan's Greener Homes ccASHP criterion all define exactly this ratio, the last as (Max −15 °C) / (Rated 8.3 °C) ≥ 70%.

The two axes are near-independent (correlation −0.168), so all nine cells describe genuinely different equipment. Each cell is then split into four capacity bands (<18k, 18–30k, 30–42k, ≥42k Btu/h), and the most frequently installed currently-certified model in each is taken as the representative — 36 in total, every one traceable to an AHRI number you can look up yourself.

A higher COP number is not a better heat pump. The > 2.0 band contains the most models but the fewest installations, and is the band most concentrated in the worst capacity-maintenance column — high reported COP tends to flag older or conservatively-rated units. Capacity maintenance is the axis that tracks real cold-climate capability.
Ratings are measured on two different bases, and this matters. The 5 °F figures are taken at maximum compressor output; the 47 °F and 17 °F figures are rated (full-load) points. That is why nearly half of all certified models report a 5 °F capacity above their 17 °F capacity — it is correct, not an error, and filtering it out would discard roughly 180,000 records of valid data. Because both bucketing numbers come from that same maximum-output test, an optimistically reported unit moves on both axes at once.
What the certificate cannot tell us. AHRI publishes capacity at three temperatures but COP at only one, and no compressor lock-out temperature — not enough to simulate a heating season. So certification is used only to choose the representative units; their actual capacity-and-power curves come from each unit's published manufacturer datasheet. The better submittals carry a full extended ratings table — for the most-installed unit in this dataset (GREE GUD36W/A-D(U)) that is 23 operating points from −30 °C to +24 °C, each giving capacity, COP and electrical power draw, plus the published heating lock-out of −30 °C. Where a manufacturer publishes no such table, the NEEP cold-climate listing is used as the fallback, cited per model. Where a datasheet and its certificate disagree — for that GREE unit, 0.79 vs 0.80 capacity maintenance and COP 1.83 vs 1.80 — the certificate governs which bucket a unit sits in and the datasheet governs the simulation, so units cannot silently drift between buckets when better data arrives.
Coverage. About 28% of records are for models with no certified 5 °F rating at all and cannot be bucketed. That group is 99% discontinued or delisted and none of it carries a cold-climate designation — it is genuinely older equipment that predates the rating, not missing data we could go and find. Counts throughout are EnerGuide record appearances, not installed units: a home audited twice counts twice, and retrofit and new-construction records are pooled. It is a sound measure of popularity and a poor headcount.

Grid emissions — the biggest choice

Hourly grid intensity comes from a temperature × hour-of-day × season "surface" built from 2019–2026 IESO / AESO / Hydro-Québec generation, calibrated against published provincial intensities (within ±6%). It is decomposed as EF = annual_level × shape, so the shape (weather/time pattern) is applied on top of the most recent complete year's level (the current grid).

  • Marginal (default): new load is served by the marginal generator — modelled as gas whenever gas is running (~500 g CO₂e/kWh in ON/AB). This is the honest basis for a new load like a heat pump.
  • Hourly average: the fleet-average intensity from the surface (ON ≈ 97, AB ≈ 414, QC ≈ 0.02 g/kWh in 2025). Appropriate for accounting existing consumption, but it understates the emissions of adding load in a gas-on-the-margin grid.
  • ECCC yearly: the province's published National-Inventory annual grid average (a single flat number: from Environment and Climate Change Canada data via StatCan 38-10-0097 ÷ 25-10-0015; validates nationally to ECCC's ~100 g/kWh headline for 2022). This is the official inventory basis, and the only option for provinces without an hourly pipeline (BC, MB, SK, NS, NB…), which also carry a flat, clearly-labelled marginal estimate. It differs from our Hourly-average number by scope — ON reads ~10–20% below The Atmospheric Fund's factor because that attributes all grid emissions to gas.
Ontario's average intensity is actively rising as gas generation grows. Quebec's marginal figure is a known gap: new winter demand there may in practice be met by higher-carbon imports (~35 g/kWh) rather than curtailed hydro — not modelled, so Quebec's "after" number is a floor.

Lifecycle terms (adjustable)

  • Combustion: natural gas 181, oil 275, propane 214 g CO₂e/kWh of fuel input (ECCC National Inventory Report).
  • Refrigerant: annual in-service leak (adjustable, default 2%/yr) + amortized end-of-life loss (15% of charge over a 15-yr life) × global-warming potential of the selected refrigerant.
  • Upstream methane: a slider (0–5%, default 1%) for gas leaked well-to-meter before combustion × a methane global-warming potential toggle — 28 (100-yr, IPCC AR5) or 82.5 (20-yr fossil methane, IPCC AR6, weighting near-term warming ≈3× higher). Labelled presets bracket the evidence: NIR-implied (~1%, Canada's National Inventory Report), measurement-adjusted (~1.5×, atmospheric-measurement studies find inventories understate leakage), and high / full-chain (~3%, adding post-meter building leakage measured at 2.0–2.5× the inventory end-use factor). A 3% full-chain leak at the 20-yr GWP roughly doubles gas heating's effective carbon intensity (≈×1.9).
  • Grid line losses: default 5% added to delivered electricity — World Bank/IEA transmission-and-distribution losses for Canada were ~5% in 2019–2020 (easing toward ~4% recently); utility figures span ~4% (transmission only) to ~10% (full path to a home). We deliberately do not use ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager's 2.05 source–site ratio: it also bundles power-plant conversion losses, which our per-kWh-generated grid factor already includes — applying it would double-count.

How accurate is this? — validation

We checked the tool's annual results against published Canadian studies — NRCan / CanmetENERGY's cold-climate heat-pump report (2022), the Canadian Climate Institute's Heat Pumps Pay Off (2023), and Efficiency Canada's Heat Pump Myth Buster (2023). The tool lands in the same territory on every headline number:

  • Seasonal efficiency: the tool's seasonal COP of ≈ 2.3–2.5 (Ottawa, Toronto, Montreal, Calgary; premium unit) matches NRCan/field values of ≈ 2.4.
  • Gas → heat pump: on the average grid the tool cuts emissions ≈ 68–74% in Ontario and ≈ 95% in Quebec — in line with NRCan (who used average factors). In Alberta the switch barely helps or slightly increases emissions, exactly as NRCan found.
  • Baseboard → heat pump: ≈ 50–58% less (Ontario/Alberta), inside the published 49–77% range; in Quebec the grid is already ~carbon-free, so there's essentially nothing to cut.
The single biggest reason our numbers can look lower than a headline you've read elsewhere is the marginal grid basis (our default). Most published studies price heat-pump electricity at the grid average; we default to the marginal gas generator because that's what actually runs to meet new demand — an honest but more demanding test. Flip the toggle to “Average” in Advanced to compare like-for-like. Two further gaps are deliberate: our grid is calibrated to the current 2025 mix (Ontario's has roughly tripled in carbon intensity since 2020, when older studies were written), and our homes are the median audited house of each type (larger, and higher-emitting in absolute terms, than the single reference house those studies model — though the percentage change is barely affected). Full validation tables are in HeatPump/METHODOLOGY.md.

What this does not do

No equipment or installation costs — the cost card compares annual operating cost only, not payback; no solar gains or thermostat setbacks (the balance point absorbs them); no ground-source option in this version; a single provincial grid applied to each city's own weather. Treat every number as an estimate with a band around it, not a guarantee.
