This tool runs a transparent, dependency-free hour-by-hour simulation (8,760 hours) of a single typical year, entirely in your browser. It is a screening tool: good for understanding direction and rough magnitude, not a substitute for a heat-loss assessment or a quote. Full derivations, factors and validation live in HeatPump/METHODOLOGY.md in the repository.

The calculation chain, step by step

Steps 1–7 above walk through this same chain live, with your actual numbers plugged in at every stage. Here is the arithmetic itself, in general form.

1
How much heat the home needs
heat(hour) = UA × (balance − outdoor)
Summed over every hour below the zero-heat (balance) temperature, this gives the year's total heat demand in kWh.
2
How efficiently the heat pump makes that heat
electricity = heat ÷ COP
COP is read off the selected unit's curve at that hour's outdoor temperature; below the tier's lockout the backup (if any) carries the load instead.
3
Energy bought, before and after
now: heat ÷ furnace efficiency (or heat ÷ 1.0 for baseboard) heat pump: (heat pump kWh ÷ COP) + backup kWh
The same heat, delivered two ways — burning fuel at the furnace's rated efficiency versus running the heat pump at its (varying) COP.
4
Emissions today
electric baseboard: electricity kWh × grid intensity (g/kWh) × (1 + line losses) fuel furnace: fuel kWh × combustion factor (g/kWh)
Fuel burned in the home emits directly; electricity is priced at the grid's carbon intensity.
5
Emissions with the heat pump
grid electricity: HP kWh × grid intensity × (1 + line losses) + backup fuel: backup kWh × combustion factor (if dual-fuel) + refrigerant: leak + end-of-life charge, amortized annually
The grid intensity is varied hour by hour on the chosen basis (marginal / hourly average / annual) and weighted to the hours when the pump actually runs.
6
What it costs to run
now: (fuel or electricity) × current rate + fixed charges heat pump: (HP electricity + backup fuel) × current rates + fixed charges
Each scenario's yearly energy is priced at the province's current residential rates. The number to trust is the difference between the two — charges identical in both (like the electricity service charge) cancel out.

Heating load

Each hour's heat demand is Load = UA × max(0, T_balance − T_outdoor). Design heat load (kW, at the city's design temperature) and zero-heat temperature (°C) are direct inputs you set — UA = design_load_kW × 1000 / (T_balance − T_design_city), where the city's design temperature comes from NRCan/HOT2000 weather-station records (see HeatPump/data/processed/city_design_temps.json). There is no auto-sizing: earlier versions of this tool inferred a design load from an archetype label (home vintage/type); that inference step has been removed in favour of letting you enter your own EnerGuide/HOT2000 design heat loss, or a number calibrated against the 4 audited-home reference medians shown beside the slider. Domestic hot water is excluded.

Weather

A CWEC2020 Typical Meteorological Year (8,760 hourly temperatures) for each city's airport station (ECCC engineering climate datasets).

Heat-pump performance

Tier × size selects exactly one of 9 real, individually-certified units (3 performance tiers × 3 capacity bands — <18k, 18–30k, 30–42k Btu/h) — no scaling, no averaging, no interpolation between models. Capacity and COP versus outdoor temperature are digitized from that unit's own primary public manufacturer datasheet (submittal / product data), at maximum heating output, converted to SI and normalized on its AHRI-certified rated (47 °F) capacity. The equipment-curve chart and the calculation walkthrough name the exact brand/model and AHRI number in play.

Below-lockout is now a fixed, assumed hard-stop by tier — not the unit's own published minimum. Real published minimums for the 9 certified units span −15 to −30 °C, scattered by model rather than cleanly by tier. Rather than expose a per-unit lockout plus a hard-stop/continue-derated toggle (the previous version's approach), this tool now assumes a single round number per tier — low −15 °C, mid −20 °C, high −25 °C — and hard-stops there: output is zero below it, no derating. This is a simplification in the spirit of the tiers themselves (see “Don't overcomplicate” in the project's working notes): easier to state and defend than a per-unit figure, at the cost of not reflecting the specific selected model's own real cold-weather floor. Open question, not a settled result: is −15/−20/−25 °C the right set of cutoffs? These are round-number placeholders chosen to sit inside each tier's real published range, not fitted to it — the scatter/curve data in “Heat-pump selection: the underlying data” below is what any revision of these numbers would be argued from. The switch-over temperature control (Advanced → Backup heat) is separate: it decides when a dual-fuel system chooses to hand off to its furnace, and can be set warmer than the tier cut-off; below the tier cut-off the heat pump can't run regardless of that setting.
A sizing sweep card re-runs the whole year at 40–160% of your design load, synthetically scaling the selected unit's own capacity curve — a hypothetical resizing for sensitivity, not a menu of purchasable sizes (real units come only in the 9 discrete tier × band combinations above). It has no short-cycling model, so it understates the penalty of oversizing (short-cycling and worse part-load efficiency) — oversizing looks nearly free on that chart but isn't in a real home. A quick check of ERS retrofit records (installed-capacity vs. audited design heat loss for homes with a certified heat pump) found real installs scattered widely around design load — median ratio ≈0.66, only 24% within ±20% — so no single "correctly sized" ratio is implied or suggested by this tool.
The tiers differ mainly in cold-weather capability — premium-tier units tend to hold capacity better and lock out colder than value-tier units, but this varies unit to unit; check the specific model named for your selection rather than assuming tier behaviour in general.

How the equipment is chosen — rebuilt 2026

The tiers above replaced an earlier scheme that averaged two hand-picked, vendor-list models per tier, with tier boundaries of our own invention. The current method separates two jobs that should never have been one: AHRI certification is the sampling frame; manufacturer datasheets are the measurement.

Which models matter comes from Canada's own EnerGuide/ERS audit records — every AHRI certified reference number ever entered by an energy advisor, 439,975 record appearances across 15,148 distinct certified models. So the answer to “why these heat pumps?” is simply: these are the ones Canadians actually bought. Not a vendor list, not a hand-picked shortlist.

How they are bucketed uses two ratings from each unit's public AHRI certificate, on a 3×3 grid:

  • COP at 5 °F — ≤ 1.8 / 1.8–2.0 / > 2.0. Efficiency in deep cold. The bands are deliberately coarse: 1.75 is the certification floor shared by ENERGY STAR, CEE and NEEP, and 55% of records report exactly 1.80, so this number measures compliance as much as performance and should not be read as a fine-grained ranking.
  • Capacity maintenance — capacity at 5 °F ÷ capacity at 47 °F, banded < 0.60 / 0.60–0.80 / ≥ 0.80. How much heat the unit still delivers when you need it most. This is not our metric: ENERGY STAR v6.2, CEE and NRCan's Greener Homes ccASHP criterion all define exactly this ratio, the last as (Max −15 °C) / (Rated 8.3 °C) ≥ 70%.

The two axes are near-independent (correlation −0.168), so all nine cells describe genuinely different equipment. Each cell is then split into four capacity bands (<18k, 18–30k, 30–42k, ≥42k Btu/h), and the most frequently installed currently-certified model in each is taken as the representative — 36 in total, every one traceable to an AHRI number you can look up yourself. This tool currently simulates the 9 cells in the <18k/18–30k/30–42k bands (the ≥42k band is identified but not yet wired into the simulation).

A higher COP number is not a better heat pump. The > 2.0 band contains the most models but the fewest installations, and is the band most concentrated in the worst capacity-maintenance column — high reported COP tends to flag older or conservatively-rated units. Capacity maintenance is the axis that tracks real cold-climate capability.
Ratings are measured on two different bases, and this matters. The 5 °F figures are taken at maximum compressor output; the 47 °F and 17 °F figures are rated (full-load) points. That is why nearly half of all certified models report a 5 °F capacity above their 17 °F capacity — it is correct, not an error, and filtering it out would discard roughly 180,000 records of valid data. Because both bucketing numbers come from that same maximum-output test, an optimistically reported unit moves on both axes at once.
What the certificate cannot tell us. AHRI publishes capacity at three temperatures but COP at only one, and no compressor lock-out temperature — not enough to simulate a heating season. So certification is used only to choose the representative units; their actual capacity-and-power curves come from each unit's published manufacturer datasheet. The better submittals carry a full extended ratings table — for the most-installed unit in this dataset (GREE GUD36W/A-D(U)) that is 23 operating points from −30 °C to +24 °C, each giving capacity, COP and electrical power draw, plus the published heating lock-out of −30 °C. Where a manufacturer publishes no such table, the NEEP cold-climate listing is used as the fallback, cited per model. Where a datasheet and its certificate disagree — for that GREE unit, 0.79 vs 0.80 capacity maintenance and COP 1.83 vs 1.80 — the certificate governs which bucket a unit sits in and the datasheet governs the simulation, so units cannot silently drift between buckets when better data arrives.
Coverage. About 28% of records are for models with no certified 5 °F rating at all and cannot be bucketed. That group is 99% discontinued or delisted and none of it carries a cold-climate designation — it is genuinely older equipment that predates the rating, not missing data we could go and find. Counts throughout are EnerGuide record appearances, not installed units: a home audited twice counts twice, and retrofit and new-construction records are pooled. It is a sound measure of popularity and a poor headcount.

Grid emissions — the biggest choice

Hourly grid intensity comes from a temperature × hour-of-day × season "surface" built from 2019–2026 IESO / AESO / Hydro-Québec generation, calibrated against published provincial intensities (within ±6%). It is decomposed as EF = annual_level × shape, so the shape (weather/time pattern) is applied on top of the most recent complete year's level (the current grid). Of every input on this page, which of the three bases below you price electricity at moves the verdict the most — on a gas-on-the-margin grid, switching from Marginal to Hourly average alone can flip a heat pump from raising emissions to cutting them. The Grid emissions basis toggle in the controls switches it directly; every number and chart on the page updates.

  • Marginal (default): new load is served by the marginal generator — modelled as gas whenever gas is running (~500 g CO₂e/kWh in ON/AB). This is the honest basis for a new load like a heat pump.
  • Hourly average: the fleet-average intensity from the surface (ON ≈ 97, AB ≈ 414, QC ≈ 0.02 g/kWh in 2025). Appropriate for accounting existing consumption, but it understates the emissions of adding load in a gas-on-the-margin grid.
  • ECCC yearly: the province's published National-Inventory annual grid average (a single flat number: from Environment and Climate Change Canada data via StatCan 38-10-0097 ÷ 25-10-0015; validates nationally to ECCC's ~100 g/kWh headline for 2022). This is the official inventory basis, and the only option for provinces without an hourly pipeline (BC, MB, SK, NS, NB…), which also carry a flat, clearly-labelled marginal estimate. It differs from our Hourly-average number by scope — ON reads ~10–20% below The Atmospheric Fund's factor because that attributes all grid emissions to gas.
Ontario's average intensity is actively rising as gas generation grows. Quebec's marginal figure is a known gap: new winter demand there may in practice be met by higher-carbon imports (~35 g/kWh) rather than curtailed hydro — not modelled, so Quebec's "after" number is a floor.

Lifecycle terms (adjustable)

  • Combustion: natural gas 181, oil 275, propane 214 g CO₂e/kWh of fuel input (ECCC National Inventory Report).
  • Refrigerant: annual in-service leak (adjustable, default 2%/yr) + amortized end-of-life loss (15% of charge over a 15-yr life) × global-warming potential and factory charge mass, both now per-refrigerant (corrected 2026-08-12 — previously a single GWP set and a flat charge-per-kW ratio applied to all four options regardless of which was selected). GWP100: R-410A 2256, R-32 771, R-454B 531, R-290 0.02 (IPCC AR6 WG1 Ch.7 Table 7.SM.7, blend-weighted from constituent molecules — e.g. R-410A = 50% R-32 + 50% R-125). Charge mass: 0.250 kg/kW (R-410A) · 0.236 (R-454B) · 0.160 (R-32) · 0.035 (R-290) — R-410A's figure is this tool's own original per-kW baseline; the other three scale it by the charge-mass *ratio* a peer tool (Building Decarbonization Alliance's Heat Pump Lifecycle Emissions Explorer) reports for a fixed reference unit (R-410A 3.6kg : R-454B 3.4kg : R-32 2.3kg : R-290 0.5kg) — since that reference unit's own capacity isn't stated, only the ratio between refrigerants carries over, not the absolute kg figures. R-290 (propane)'s low ratio matches reality: flammability limits cap its allowable charge by code, not just its lower density. Uncertainty: this ratio-scaling is an approximation, not an independently-sourced per-refrigerant charge curve — see HeatPump/BDA_COMPARISON.md and METHODOLOGY.md.
  • Upstream & grid losses (Yes/No): a single switch now stands in for two previously independent sliders — an upstream "methane-equivalent" term before combustion, and grid transmission/distribution line losses added to delivered electricity. When set to Yes (default) it applies one fixed value for each rather than a range. The upstream term is calibrated to The Atmospheric Fund's Fugitive Methane guideline (May 2022): TAF publishes +65% as the carbon-intensity increase over combustion-only for a single home's gas-to-heat-pump project (their "no pipeline infrastructure change" scenario — the right match for this tool, vs. their higher +92% "infrastructure change" scenario for neighbourhood-scale conversions), using the 20-year methane GWP (85) per TAF's own guidance that "modelling activities that impact natural gas consumption" should use GWP20, not the 100-year GWP appropriate for emissions inventories. TAF attributes that +65% to two effects this engine can't represent separately — fugitive methane leakage (+10 points) and non-methane extraction "process emissions" like flaring and venting (+55 points) — so rather than pick one, the engine's single methane-leak lever is set to 2.14% (at GWP 85), a calibrated value chosen so leak% × GWP reproduces TAF's +65% ratio in aggregate. This is a bundled proxy, not a literal reading of how much gas physically leaks. Line loss is set at 5% (World Bank/IEA Canada transmission-and-distribution losses were ~5% in 2019–2020, easing toward ~4% in 2021–2023 before ticking back up to ~4.2% in 2024; utility figures span ~4% transmission-only to ~10% full path to a home — we deliberately don't use ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager's 1.83 source–site ratio for Canadian grid electricity, since it also bundles power-plant conversion losses our per-kWh-generated grid factor already includes). Setting it to No zeroes both terms. Open to-do, not a settled simplification: collapsing two independently-evidenced rates into one on/off switch loses the ability to reason about them separately (e.g. a high-leakage gas region with average grid losses), and the methane/process-emissions bundling means the upstream term can't be read as a leak rate on its own — reinstating adjustable rates, or at least a low/mid/high preset per term (and ideally separating process emissions from methane), is tracked in ROADMAP.md.

Where upstream methane comes from — the map

Source: NASA GES DISC's Global Fuel Exploitation Inventory (GFEI) CH₄, v1, 2016 (Scarpelli et al. 2020, essd-12-563-2020; DOI 10.5067/Q28GFYJYFZ7H). A global 0.1° × 0.1° grid of fugitive methane emissions (IPCC 2006 category 1B2 — fuel exploitation, not combustion) built from countries' own UNFCCC-reported totals where available, IPCC 2006 default methods elsewhere, spatially allocated to known infrastructure locations: mines, wells, pipelines, compressor stations, storage facilities, processing plants and refineries. All of those are summed into one number per fuel type — the map cannot and does not isolate pipeline transmission losses specifically, despite "pipeline" being one of the infrastructure classes it draws on.

The page's own extract (Python/gfei_ch4_extract.py) subsets the global grid to Canada and block-averages it to a coarser 0.5° grid (mean of the 5×5 source cells per output cell — density is an intensive quantity, so no area-weighting is needed) purely to keep the shipped JSON small (~5,200 nonzero cells, ~180 KB); it changes resolution, not values. The map's colour scale is log-scaled per selected layer (all-sources / gas / oil / coal), since raw emission density is extremely right-skewed — most cells are near zero with a small number of intense clusters (Alberta and BC gas fields chief among them for Canada).

2016 is the newest vintage available at this scope — we looked for a more recent equivalent and didn't find one; this map should be read as "the last time this was measured this way," not current-year reality. It is a gridded estimate, not a direct measurement or a Canadian regulatory inventory — treat it as illustrative of where fugitive methane clusters, not a precise current-day number for any one facility or region. NASA/US-federal data products are public-domain and freely redistributable, which is why this map (unlike some other third-party sources this project has had to avoid) can be shipped directly rather than only linked.

We checked the tool's annual results against published Canadian studies — NRCan / CanmetENERGY's cold-climate heat-pump report (2022), the Canadian Climate Institute's Heat Pumps Pay Off (2023), and Efficiency Canada's Heat Pump Myth Buster (2023). The tool lands in the same territory on every headline number:

  • Seasonal efficiency: the tool's seasonal COP of ≈ 2.3–2.5 (Ottawa, Toronto, Montreal, Calgary; premium unit) matches NRCan/field values of ≈ 2.4.
  • Gas → heat pump: on the average grid the tool cuts emissions ≈ 68–74% in Ontario and ≈ 95% in Quebec — in line with NRCan (who used average factors). In Alberta the switch barely helps or slightly increases emissions, exactly as NRCan found.
  • Baseboard → heat pump: ≈ 50–58% less (Ontario/Alberta), inside the published 49–77% range; in Quebec the grid is already ~carbon-free, so there's essentially nothing to cut.
The single biggest reason our numbers can look lower than a headline you've read elsewhere is the marginal grid basis (our default). Most published studies price heat-pump electricity at the grid average; we default to the marginal gas generator because that's what actually runs to meet new demand — an honest but more demanding test. Flip the toggle to “Average” in Advanced to compare like-for-like. Two further gaps are deliberate: our grid is calibrated to the current 2025 mix (Ontario's has roughly tripled in carbon intensity since 2020, when older studies were written), and our homes are the median audited house of each type (larger, and higher-emitting in absolute terms, than the single reference house those studies model — though the percentage change is barely affected). Full validation tables are in HeatPump/METHODOLOGY.md.

Real homes explorer — how the balance point is derived

The "real homes" section above plots every ERS-audited home in the selected city (pre-retrofit condition), not a sample or a model archetype — up to ~290,000 homes per city. Peak load comes straight from each home's own audit (design heat loss at the city's design temperature, EGHDESHTLOSS). Annual heat delivered does not — the audit records energy consumed by the heating system (EGHFURNACEAEC), which only equals heat delivered for electric-resistance heat. Converted using the system's own seasonal efficiency (EGHFURSEASEFF, an AFUE-style percentage, >100 for a heat pump): delivered = consumed × (efficiency / 100). Skipping this conversion originally let a handful of homes whose pre-retrofit system was already a heat pump masquerade as the most efficient homes in the city, purely because a COP well above 1 makes consumed energy look small next to a normal peak load — caught on an Ottawa ground-source-heat-pump home whose "best in the city" ranking dropped once its delivered heat was corrected up ~2.3×. Records with no plausible efficiency value (outside 30–400%) are excluded and counted in each city's totals, not silently kept — this is why Quebec City and Montreal, in particular, show noticeably fewer solved homes than other cities. The balance point is derived, not measured: assuming a straight-line relationship between outdoor temperature and heating load with no internal or solar gains, it is the outdoor temperature at which that home's own peak-load line, integrated over a full TMY year, would reproduce its own delivered annual energy. Because real gains are ignored by construction, this "balance point" is best read as effective — it absorbs real internal/solar gains, occupant behaviour and TMY-vs-actual-year weather deviation into one number, not a physical measurement of any one of them.

Worst/best avoid a single miskeyed ERS row stretching the display: each home is ranked on the lower of its own peak-load percentile and annual-heat-delivered percentile within the filtered set (so a home has to be extreme on both axes to rank as bad), and "Worst"/"Best" are the 99th/1st percentile of that ranking — not the literal maximum/minimum. "Average" is the arithmetic mean of the filtered set and is not any single real home.

What this does not do

No equipment or installation costs — the cost card compares annual operating cost only, not payback; no solar gains or thermostat setbacks (the zero-heat temperature absorbs them); no ground-source option in this version; a single provincial grid applied to each city's own weather. Treat every number as an estimate with a band around it, not a guarantee.
