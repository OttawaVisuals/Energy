Assumptions & methodology

Every number above is a modelled estimate — here's exactly how it's built, and what to distrust. This page is a spinoff of the Heat Pump Explorer's optional "potential AC" scenario, promoted to its own page; nothing here feeds back into that page's heating numbers, or vice versa.

Show assumptions — how this is calculated, and what to distrust

This tool runs an hour-by-hour simulation (8,760 hours, one typical summer) in your browser — no server, no dependencies. It is a screening tool: useful for direction and rough magnitude, not a substitute for a load calculation or a contractor's quote.

How the cooling load is estimated

No real cooling data is looked up for this scenario — there is no specific house selected here, just a design heat load (the same input the Heat Pump Explorer uses for its heating simulation). Instead, two correlations found across 209,272 real ERS-audited homes (pooled across 14 tracked cities) turn that design heat load into a plausible cooling profile:

  • Peak cooling from peak heating: cool_peak_kW = 0.2789 × heat_peak_kW + 1.3254 (r = 0.683, R² = 0.467). Physically real, not coincidence — both peaks are driven by the same building envelope (UA, floor area).
  • Annual cooling energy from peak cooling: annual_cool_kWh = 838.3 × cool_peak_kW (a through-origin ratio, r = 0.778). Annual heating and cooling energy do not correlate well with each other (r ≈ 0.2–0.25) — annual energy is dominated by climate-hours and occupant behaviour, which heating and cooling don't share, but peak load in both is dominated by the shared envelope, which is why this second relationship works so much better than heating-energy-to-cooling-energy would.

The cooling balance point (the outdoor temperature above which the home needs cooling) is then solved the same way a heating balance point is: cooling-degree-hours are integrated against this city's own weather year until they match the estimated annual energy, anchored on the fixed 25 °C HOT2000 indoor cooling setpoint (ThermostatCooling — a program constant, confirmed zero-variance across 620,107 real audit records, not an assumption standing in for a missing field).

These are always estimated fields — two correlations removed from measurement (a correlation-estimated peak, run through a ratio-estimated energy, run through a balance-point solve). They are not shown with the confidence of a real per-house measurement, and are not intended to be: their entire purpose is giving a home with no installed AC — the population a "what would adding AC cost" question is actually about — a plausible cooling profile at all.
Cooling curves — spec-sheet interpolation

Both the standard AC's and every heat pump cell's cooling-mode capacity/COP curve are built exactly the same way the Heat Pump Explorer's heating side already builds its curves: real points read off the manufacturer's own spec sheet at the standard AHRI cooling test condition (80 °F DB / 67 °F WB indoor), linearly interpolated between published points, and held flat outside the published range rather than extrapolated. No synthetic temperature-response shape, no badge-derived (SEER2/EER2) reconstruction, and no multi-speed dispatch — one set of real numbers per unit, the same way heating already works.

When the equipment is undersized

Neither piece of equipment is scaled to the home's estimated peak — by design, matching the rest of this page's "no archetype auto-sizing" stance (see above). The engine handles an hour where the load exceeds the equipment's capacity by running flat-out there (delivered = min(load, capacity)) and pricing/emitting exactly that — a maxed-out unit really does draw exactly this much electricity, so the kWh/GHG/cost figures stay accurate for those hours. What's not represented is comfort: the home doesn't reach setpoint that hour, and this model has no cost or discomfort penalty for that shortfall.

This shows up most in mild-summer cities: the cooling balance point is solved against a fixed 25 °C indoor setpoint, so UA_cool = peak_kW ÷ (design_cooling_temp − 25) — for a city whose design cooling temperature sits only slightly above 25 °C (Vancouver, ≈25.7 °C, is the worst case among the 14 tracked cities), that denominator is small and UA_cool is large, so the load line climbs steeply just past the balance point while equipment capacity stays close to flat (real cooling capacity barely changes with outdoor temperature). The two cross at some point, and the shortfall grows quickly above it.

When either unit crosses this line, a red shaded zone appears on the Step 2 chart, a "Hours unmet" KPI reports what share of the cooling season is affected, and every stat card for that unit is flagged "undersized above X °C." If you don't see any of that, the selected equipment covers the full estimated load for this city/design-load combination.
Refrigerant leakage (standard AC only)

The Goodman GLXS4B spec sheet (SS-GLXS4B-R32, "Product Specifications" table) states the refrigerant directly: R-32, factory charged for 15' of liquid line. The 36,000 Btu/h (3-ton) unit modelled here — GLXS4BA3610A* — is listed at 69 oz (≈1.96 kg). This is a real spec-sheet figure for the exact unit used, not an estimate.

Annual leakage uses the same amortization the Heat Pump Explorer's heating side uses: an adjustable in-service leak rate (default 2%/yr, "Refrigerant leak rate" slider above) plus a fixed 15%-of-charge end-of-life loss spread over a 15-year life, weighted by R-32's GWP100 (771, IPCC AR6 WG1 Ch.7 Table 7.SM.7) — at the default rate, 1.96 kg × (0.02 + 0.15⁄15) × 771 ≈ 45 kg CO₂e/yr. Shown as its own segment in Step 6's emissions chart and its own line in the Standard AC stat card, rather than hidden inside "electricity."

Not added to the heat pump's cooling-mode comparison — that would double-count against the same physical unit's refrigerant charge already priced into the Heat Pump Explorer's heating-side numbers for a real heat pump, and no per-model charge mass has been sourced for the Tosot/GREE/Fujitsu cooling cells this page draws on. It also isn't part of the by-temperature/weighted/daily/hourly detail charts in Step 6 — leakage isn't tied to a particular operating hour, so it's a flat annual addition to the totals only, the same convention the Heat Pump Explorer's own refrigerant term already uses.

Cost

  • Added electricity priced per hour against each province's current residential rates. Source: canada-utility-rates, from the utilities' own published tariffs.
  • Ontario: default Time-of-use plan, priced per (month, hour). Quebec: Hydro-Québec Rate D's tier-2 (marginal/top-tier) rate.
  • The electricity fixed monthly service charge is identical with or without AC and excluded from the numbers here.
  • Federal consumer carbon charge excluded (set to zero 2025-04-01). Figures are pre-tax.
BC, Manitoba, Nova Scotia, Saskatchewan and the Atlantic provinces have no modelled rate entry yet — their cost section degrades to "rates unavailable" while electricity and GHG stay available.

What this does not do

  • No equipment or installation cost — only annual operating cost, not payback.
  • No humidity/latent-load modelling — sensible cooling load only, same simplification as the heating side's zero-heat temperature standing in for gains.
  • No refrigerant-leakage term for the heat pump's cooling mode (only the standard AC has one — see caveat above).
  • No cost or discomfort penalty when equipment is undersized — see "When the equipment is undersized" above.
  • This is a standalone spinoff of the Heat Pump Explorer's "potential AC" add-on — the two pages don't share live state; changing a value here doesn't affect heatpump.html.

Every number here is a modelled estimate with a margin of error, not a guarantee.
