What does a new kilowatt of winter peak cost?

When more homes heat with electricity, the grid has to meet a higher winter peak. Here we take one simple rule, every added kilowatt is met with new generation plus new wires, and price it using Ontario's own planning numbers, NREL's technology costs, and a utility study of the grid upgrades. Then we ask what the peak costs per unit of electricity sold.

7 · Peak versus utilization

A kilowatt of peak capacity costs the same whether it runs one hour or all year, so the cost per kilowatt-hour sold depends on how many hours it sells. Utilities want a low peak but a high load factor (average demand ÷ peak demand). Electric heating tends to do the opposite.

How this works, in plain language

We start from one simple idea: if homes that burned gas switch to electric heat pumps, they add to the coldest-evening demand on the grid. For each extra kilowatt of that peak, someone has to build a power plant (or a battery) and the wires and land to deliver it.

The plant. We take what the IESO and NREL say it costs to build each kind of plant, per kilowatt. Then we ask how much of a plant you can count on at the winter peak. A gas plant is close to 90% available, a wind farm about a quarter, a battery about half, solar none. The cost per winter-peak kilowatt is the plant cost divided by that share.

The wires and land. FortisBC studied Kelowna and estimated what upgrades and land would cost if its gas heat moved to electricity. We divide their total by the extra peak they modelled. You can switch to other published numbers.

One-time or yearly. The one-time view is the investment needed when the peak arrives. The yearly view spreads that investment over the plant's life at the discount rate you choose.

Cost per kWh. Peak capacity is paid for once per kilowatt, so the more hours a year that kilowatt is used, the cheaper each kilowatt-hour. That is why a flat load is cheap to serve and a winter-heating-only load is not.

These are planning-level numbers from public sources, not a forecast of anyone's bill. Where a number is our own arithmetic, the page says so. For the full list of assumptions see the advanced section.

Advanced methodology — formulas, sources & caveats

Sources and vintage

IESO 2026 Annual Planning Outlook (report 2026-03-20; capacity-expansion modules 2026-09): Annual-Planning-Outlook-Data-Tables.xlsx (Figures 2, 5–8); Capacity-Expansion-Scenario-and-Outcomes-Data.xlsx (Figures 1–3, Levelized Cost and Components, Elec Cost by Component); Marginal-Energy-Costs-and-Capacity-Costs.xlsx (Table 1 capacity cost); Fuel-Cost.xlsx (USD/CAD 0.73–0.75). IESO levelized costs are 2025 CAD per nameplate kW; system costs are nominal. NREL 2024 ATB v4 workbook (data.openei.org/submissions/6006, CC BY 4.0), sheets Summary_CAPEX, Summary_FOM; Moderate cost case, R&D financial case (no tax credits), 2022 USD.

Formulas

Annualizing: CRF = r / (1 − (1+r)−n). NREL annual cost per nameplate kW = CAPEX × FX × CRF(r, n) + FOM × FX, with n = 30 (NREL's recovery period). IESO's annual figure is its own published Levelized Cost ($/kW-yr), which embeds IESO's financing and tax-credit assumptions and a 20–75-year economic life by plant type, so the two annual figures are not on an identical financial basis. Wind and hydro show the median across IESO's zones. Wires and land annual = one-time × CRF(r, tdlife).

Winter-peak cost per kW = (plant cost per nameplate kW ÷ winter share) + wires and land per kW. Winter share defaults to IESO winter effective capacity 2026–27 ÷ 2026 installed capacity, by fuel (Figures 7 and 8). Storage is IESO's own storage line (0.6 of 1.2 GW). Variable fuel and O&M are not in the capacity cost.

FortisBC per added kW = (Table 4-2 upgrades + Table 4-3 land) ÷ (case peak − 472 MW), where 472 MW is Kelowna's 2040 winter peak with zero electrification (Fortis Figure 3-3). Per-kW values (about $5,300–7,100, $4,500–6,100 and $2,700–3,600 for low–high land) are our arithmetic, not Fortis's. Cost steps at 550 MW and 950 MW (new 500 kV lines), so per-kW falls with size.

Cost per MWh of peak = annual $/kW-yr ÷ (8.76 × load factor). Ontario's system load factor is mean ÷ peak of IESO monthly demand for complete calendar years in grid_json/grid_on_history.json (Python/grid_history_etl.py); it ranges 0.66–0.70 for 2021–2025.

Measured vs modelled vs assumed

Measured: Ontario demand history (load factor). Planning data: IESO capacity and cost forecasts. Literature: NREL/IPCC technology costs and lifecycle greenhouse gases. Assumed: winter shares (editable), the 35% heating-only load factor (illustrative until real hourly data replaces it), annualizing rate and life. Ours: per-kW Fortis conversion, build-out capital estimate (new MW × overnight cost in the chosen year, undiscounted, constant 2025 CAD, ignores transmission-to-site and demand response).

Limits

What the data can't tell us: hourly load shapes of electrified homes, real cold-climate heat pump performance, and any Ontario-specific wires cost per kW. What we assumed: every added kW needs new capacity (true only once spare capacity is gone; IESO says Ontario needs new capacity in any case); Kelowna's wires costs transfer to other places (they will not: the filing is for a capacity-short city with agricultural-land limits and was prepared by a gas utility); the Dunsky Ontario value is an annual avoided cost and not a capital cost. FortisBC's wires costs exclude upgrades to BC Hydro's system and Indigenous consultation. The IESO's own cost of capacity assumes the winter-peaking years only from 2042. What would change the answer: hourly utility data for the load factor, lab heat pump curves for the peak itself, a lower-cost local wires estimate, or a different marginal plant.

Not reported: lifecycle greenhouse gases for simple-cycle gas (neither NREL nor IPCC reports it separately). Pipeline: Python/costofpeak_etl.py → costofpeak_json/costofpeak.json. Project doc: docs/COSTOFPEAK.md.
