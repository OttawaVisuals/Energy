Average construction time, months

In plain terms: across Canada's big metro areas, a single-detached house took about 5 months from foundation to finish in 1990 and about 9 months in 2025; an apartment building went from about 11 months to about 23. The "all homes" line rose faster still (about 8 → 19 months), partly because more of what gets built now is apartments. Points based on fewer than 20 finished homes are left out, so small places and rare types show gaps rather than noise. Source: CMHC Starts and Completions Survey, read from CMHC's Housing Market Information Portal. Not available from Statistics Canada.

Method. CMHC's monthly "Average Length of Construction (in months)" (HMIP tables 1.2.7 by dwelling type, 1.2.8 by intended market; "For: Census Metropolitan Areas", MetType=Cmas), 1990-01 onward. CMHC publishes no formal definition; we read it as the mean months from start (footings poured for the whole structure) to completion (all work done, or all but 10%) over units completed in the month. 12-mo avg = Σ(monthst × completionst) ÷ Σ completionst over the trailing 12 months, weighted by CMHC completions pulled at the same coverage, so a busy month counts more than a quiet one. Each point needs ≥ 20 completions (that month, or in the 12-month window), and a 12-month point needs ≥ 6 months with data; below that it's dropped. Gaps: CMHC shows 0.0 months where no time was recorded: all of 1992–1993 everywhere, plus scattered CMA months (8,660 cells in total). A zero-month build is impossible, so these are treated as missing. CMHC's own province, Canada and "All" figures still average those zeros in, which pulls them down in the affected months (230 months across 49 series sit below every one of their categories). We show CMHC's figures as published. Coverage: province and Canada values average their CMAs only, not rural areas or towns. PEI has no CMA, so it has no data. HMIP's "Ottawa" is the Ontario part of Ottawa-Gatineau; Gatineau is the Quebec part. CMHC has collected completions only in CMAs since 2023-01-01. No reliability codes are published for this table. Pipeline: Python/construction_time_etl.py → construction_json/ctime/; catalogue and caveats in docs/HMIP_CATALOGUE.md and docs/CONSTRUCTION.md.

CMHC vs the city’s own permit records

In plain terms: the city line uses the city’s own building permits for new homes, from the day the permit was issued to the day the city closed it (or, in Edmonton, granted occupancy). That is a different clock from CMHC’s, which runs from the foundation to the finished home. Permits are often issued a little before work starts and closed after people move in, so the city number usually runs longer. Read the two lines for whether they move together, not for an exact match. Each point is the homes finished that year. Years with fewer than 20 permits are left out.

Method. New residential construction permits only (new building, ≥ 1 dwelling unit created), mapped onto CMHC’s four dwelling types from each city’s own type field. Duplexes, multiplexes and stacked townhouses count as apartments, as CMHC classes them, and so do the homes in mixed-use buildings. Secondary and backyard suites are left out and counted. Months = (end date − issue date) ÷ 30.4375 days, grouped by completion year to match CMHC. Median and Average are both weighted by dwelling units, like CMHC’s average: a 200-unit building counts 200 times. CMHC’s line is its monthly figure for the whole CMA, averaged over the calendar year and weighted by completions. Only permits whose status confirms completion are used. Calgary, Halifax and Toronto also stamp an end date on cancelled, expired, withdrawn or dormant permits, and those are excluded and counted. First year shown = first full year of the city’s issue records + the 95th-percentile build time (rounded up to whole years). Before that, only fast builds could have finished inside the records, so earlier years would read too short. Edmonton starts at 2022, when it began recording occupancy dates. Clean-up years are hidden: years in which more than a third of the permits closed had been issued over 5 years earlier. Toronto has been closing old files in bulk since 2024 (53% of its 2025 single-detached closures were 5+ years old). The table keeps their counts. All dwelling types is shown only when every type holding 10% or more of that year’s homes has enough permits of its own. Every gate count is in city_build.json. Pipeline: Python/city_build_time_etl.py; full write-up in docs/CONSTRUCTION.md.

How many buildings have each trade in the open data

Share of the buildings in the selected period that have at least one new-build permit of that trade matched to them. A trade at 0% (for example mechanical for Winnipeg houses) means the city publishes no permits of that kind for that building type. These gaps are findings about the data, not zeros.

By year the permit was issued

In plain terms: a building permit is only the start. Plumbers, electricians, gas fitters and HVAC contractors each pull their own permits, and the city inspects the building at several points. This page lines all of that up for the same building. In "Typical", each bar uses that part's own median, so the bars show typical timing across many buildings rather than one real schedule; "One building" shows a single real project. Dates are the city's records. A permit's closing date is when the city closed the file, which can be well after the work was done, so a long bar can mean waiting for paperwork as much as working. In plain terms: in Winnipeg the electrician and the plumber each pull their own permit for a new building, separate from the building permit, so they can be lined up with it. In "Typical", each bar uses that part's own median, so the bars show typical timing across many buildings rather than one real schedule; "One building" shows a single real project. Method. Python/calgary_project_timelines.py → construction_json/calgary_project_timelines.json. Buildings: City of Calgary Building Permits (c2es-76ed), workclass='New', statuscurrent='Completed', issued 2018–2026; class from housingunits and permitclassmapped. Trades: Mechanical (HVAC) cdrc-r4u8, Plumbing 5pvv-k7hn, Electrical vxgy-id5s, Gas tg24-jt7r (all workclassgroup='New'). Inspections: Building Inspections Results 8ced-xbvn joined on externalfilenum = permitnum (first date per type). Join: by address, the trade permit's originaladdress against the building's originaladdress and every address in locationaddresses (many new projects carry a lot address in the first, which is why this matters: new-construction HVAC matches went from 16% to 67%), issue dates within ±450 days, nearest building wins when several qualify. The address join is ours, not a city key, and has not been checked by hand. Per building and trade: permits matched, first issued day, last closed day, span = last closed − first issued across all matched permits (a 3+ unit building has a median of 5 HVAC permits). Summary: medians over buildings that have all four trades matched and closed, per year the building permit was issued; the default period is 2019–2024 because Calgary's trade datasets start between 2017-10 and 2018-08 (2018 trade coverage is incomplete) and 2025–2026 only contain buildings already finished, so they read short; a trade missing from the open data is treated as a gap, never as zero days; cells under 10 buildings are suppressed. HVAC share = HVAC span ÷ building permit length, median of the per-building ratios. Early-flagged: a trade permit issued more than 60 days before the building permit, or an inspection dated before its issue, suggests a re-issued or amended permit (e.g. a BP2018-… number issued in 2022). They stay in; the figure without them is shown beside the share. Gates and drops are all in the file's meta: Licence: City of Calgary Open Data, Open Government Licence – Calgary. Method. Python/winnipeg_project_timelines.py → construction_json/winnipeg_project_timelines.json. Buildings: City of Winnipeg Detailed Building Permit Data (it4w-cpf4), work_type='Construct New', status='Closed' with a final date on or after the issue date, issued 2010–2026. Types: Housing by sub_type (single-detached with attached garage, single-detached, duplex / side by side, row housing), Multi-Residential, and all non-residential. Accessory structures (garages, sheds) and other residential types are excluded and counted. Trades: Trade Permits (urbd-qygv): electrical and plumbing (residential and commercial) and mechanical (commercial, work type M1 - HVAC), new-build work types only (e.g. SFD New, Plumb New SFD, Wire New Bldg, New Multi-family Dwelling). Later work at the same address (Additional Wiring, Air Conditioner, Rewire, Lower Level…), annual and sign-only electrical permits, and fire-sprinkler or high-hazard mechanical permits are excluded and counted; a first run without this filter gave electrical spans of about 300 days on a 520-day build, which was later work being matched. Join: by street address (number + name + type), issue dates within ±450 days, nearest building wins when several qualify (counted). The address join is ours, not a city key, and has not been checked by hand. Per building and trade: permits matched, first issued day, last closed day, span = last closed − first issued. Summary: medians over buildings with every trade their type needs matched and closed (electrical and plumbing for housing; plus mechanical for multi-residential and non-residential), per year issued. Default period 2011–2018: the open data has a final date for 91–97% of electrical and plumbing permits issued 2010–2018, 82% for 2019, 40% for 2020 and about 1% from 2021 (meta.trade_closure_pct_by_year), so later buildings cannot be timed and the few that can are not typical. Row housing is not summarised: several building permits share one street address (a permit per unit, probably), a trade permit attaches to only one of them, and only 21% show any trade (98.9% of row-housing addresses do have trade permits at some date). Share of the build: mechanical span ÷ permit length where mechanical is required, electrical span ÷ permit length for housing (no HVAC permit exists for houses). Cells under 10 buildings are suppressed. Early-flagged: a trade permit issued more than 60 days before the building permit. Gates and drops are all in the file's meta: Licence: building permits Open Government Licence – Winnipeg (as used in the existing permits pipeline); trade permits Canada Open Government Licence (dataset metadata). Does HVAC take longer in bigger buildings?

In plain terms: this follows one number, how many days the heating, ventilation and cooling permits for a building stay open, from the first one being issued to the last one closing, and splits it by how many homes the building has. Small buildings are often filed as one HVAC permit per home (a median of 4 to 6 permits), while the biggest buildings usually have a single permit for the whole building, so the table also shows permits per building. Each dot is a typical (median) building for that year and size; sizes with fewer than 10 buildings in a year are left blank rather than guessed. Only buildings whose every required trade permit is closed are counted, which leaves out the slowest builds, so real spans are somewhat longer than shown.

Method. Computed in the page from the building list in the city file (buildings[]: class, units = housingunits in Calgary and dwelling_units_created in Winnipeg, issued, trades.<trade>.span). Population: the city's 3+ home residential class (Calgary "Residential 3+ units (MURB)"; Winnipeg "Multi-residential") and only buildings listed in the file, i.e. every trade needed matched and closed. Span = last HVAC (Winnipeg: mechanical) permit closed − first issued, across all matched permits. Size bands by units: 3–5, 6–11, 12–29, 30–99, 100+; cell median over buildings issued that year; cells under 10 buildings suppressed. The chart shows only the complete-year range of each city (the same period defaults as above); other years are in the table, marked with an asterisk. Selection effect: requiring every trade to be closed under-represents slow builds, and more so for recent years. Not a causal estimate: bands also differ in building type (townhouse blocks vs towers) and in how many permits are filed per building.

What these numbers can't tell us

  • What the data can't tell us. When work was physically finished: closing dates are administrative, so a long bar can be paperwork. Anything about insulation, air-sealing or the building envelope.
  • Calgary. Inspection dates are when the city inspected, not when the work was done. Why single houses lack trade permits in the open data (lot-address filing, blanket permits or dataset gaps are all possible; we did not test them).
  • Winnipeg. No inspection dates, no gas permits, and no HVAC permits for houses (mechanical is commercial and multi-residential only). The open data has a closing date for almost no trade permit issued from about 2020, so only buildings permitted through 2018 can be timed. Row housing cannot be summarised because several building permits share one address.
  • What we assumed. That the address join links the right permits, that a ±450-day window around the building permit is wide enough, and that buildings with all their trades are like those without. Only the last is likely to be false.
  • What would change the answer. A city key linking trade permits to building permits, another city publishing the same structure, or the recent years finishing. Toronto's mechanical and plumbing permits are separate records but mostly close with the building permit, so they cannot give an independent trade length. Edmonton, Vancouver, Ottawa, Halifax and Mississauga were not checked.
  • Measured, modelled, assumed. Everything on this page is measured from city and CMHC records. The only modelling is the medians and the 12-month averages. CMHC's start and completion definitions are our reading of an unpublished definition.
